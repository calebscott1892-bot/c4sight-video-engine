# C4Sight Animation Bible

This document defines the production language for C4Sight animation. It exists so episodes are built from a disciplined visual system, not from one-off scene polish.

## Brand Animation Philosophy

C4Sight explains AI clearly for everyday people and practical businesses. The animation should feel like a calm, capable teacher using a blackboard: precise enough to be trusted, warm enough to be watchable, and playful enough to make abstract ideas memorable.

The style is blackboard/chalk first. The board is the world. Chalk marks, eraser wipes, dust, simple props, and expressive but restrained characters are the vocabulary.

C4Sight is not trying to animate every spoken word. It is trying to make ideas easier to follow.

## Core Principles

- Clarity beats density.
- One main idea should dominate each shot.
- Stillness is allowed.
- A held visual can support narration for several seconds.
- Not every line needs a full visual metaphor.
- Gags should clarify tone, not distract from the lesson.
- Visuals should look intentionally drawn, never like broken placeholders.
- The audience should always know where to look.

## Pacing Principles

Use three pacing modes:

1. **Write**: a short phrase, label, arrow, or diagram draws on.
2. **React**: characters or props respond with one readable beat.
3. **Hold**: the board stays mostly still while the voiceover carries nuance.

Default beat length:

- Hook action: 2-5 seconds.
- Teaching setup: 5-8 seconds.
- Diagram build: 8-14 seconds.
- Humour reaction: 1-3 seconds.
- Thesis hold: 4-7 seconds.

Avoid constant motion. If everything moves, nothing matters.

## When To Animate

Animate when motion creates meaning:

- A question appears.
- A misconception is corrected.
- A relationship is drawn.
- A character reacts.
- A messy idea is cleared.
- A new section begins.
- A visual metaphor lands.

## When To Hold Still

Hold still when narration needs attention:

- Definitions.
- Caveats.
- Important distinctions.
- Any sentence where the exact words matter.
- Moments after a joke, so the audience can register it.
- Moments before a transition, so the board can breathe.

Stillness should feel deliberate. Keep one strong composition on screen, not a half-empty accident.

## Scene Composition Rules

- Use safe margins on all sides.
- Keep the main idea in the title or main board zone.
- Keep captions in the caption zone only.
- Use side notes sparingly.
- Do not place important text near the logo/watermark.
- Do not stack cards randomly.
- Do not let props collide with teaching text.
- Character gags belong in the hook or side zone unless they are the main beat.

## Spacing Rules

- Leave at least 90px between unrelated text groups.
- Leave at least 60px between cards and arrows.
- Keep diagrams horizontally balanced unless a deliberate left-to-right journey is needed.
- Avoid more than three major visual groups at once.
- Avoid more than seven small labels in one shot.
- If a board feels crowded, erase or pan before adding more.

## Mobile Readability Rules

Assume the video may be watched on a phone.

- Main titles should be large enough to read in a contact sheet.
- Avoid small paragraphs on the board.
- Prefer 2-5 word board phrases.
- Use captions for sentence-level narration.
- Do not put tiny labels at the very edges.
- Never reveal captions word-by-word.
- Avoid awkward partial words in QA stills.

## Character Acting Principles

The alien characters are visual punctuation, not the show’s identity.

Approved acting modes:

- Curious observer.
- Confused misunderstanding.
- Overconfident “magic” helper.
- Frozen after correction.
- Small sad exit.
- Excited return when nuance appears.

Rules:

- One character gag per beat is usually enough.
- Characters should have clear eye/mouth emotion.
- Poses should read at thumbnail size.
- Reactions should be short and timed to the lesson.
- Characters should never cover key teaching text.
- Characters should not keep moving during serious explanation unless they are quietly parked.

## Humour Principles

Humour should be clever, dry, and quick.

Good humour:

- A magic wand gets disappointed.
- An alien wizard realizes it is wrong.
- A saucepan appears as a strange but contained visual joke.
- A character reacts to a correction, then exits.

Avoid:

- Random chaos that makes the lesson hard to read.
- Long cartoon business.
- Too many props at once.
- Childish mugging.
- Jokes that imply AI is conscious.

## Transition Principles

Approved transitions:

- Chalk write-on.
- Chalk path draw.
- Eraser wipe.
- Dust puff.
- Freeze burst for a comic stop.
- Subtle board pan or push-in.

Transitions should communicate structure:

- Erase means “new idea.”
- Underline means “remember this.”
- Cross-out means “misconception.”
- Circle means “focus here.”
- Freeze means “the joke just landed.”

## Voiceover-To-Visual Relationship

The voiceover leads; the board supports.

Use visuals to:

- Establish the question.
- Name the misconception.
- Show the simple mental model.
- Add one memorable metaphor.
- Mark the takeaway.

Do not illustrate every noun. Do not add a new object just because the script mentioned it. Sometimes the strongest choice is one phrase on the board while the narration explains.

## Approved Scene Archetypes

1. **Centered Teaching Scene**  
   One title or thesis, one support drawing, optional underline.

2. **Left-Right Comparison Scene**  
   Myth vs reality, before vs after, wrong model vs better model.

3. **Hook Chaos Scene**  
   Controlled playful misunderstanding with props, cards, and one character gag.

4. **Simple Diagram Scene**  
   Three or fewer nodes connected by chalk arrows.

5. **Warning Scene**  
   One warning icon, one checklist, one corrective phrase.

6. **Quote / Thesis Scene**  
   One large sentence held while narration expands it.

7. **Roadmap Scene**  
   Three-step episode map with numbered chalk markers.

8. **Metaphor Burst Scene**  
   A short visual joke or metaphor that resolves back to teaching mode.

## Banned / Overused Behaviours

- Generic AI blobs.
- Cyberpunk or neon styling.
- Gradients as a design language.
- Glossy SaaS cards.
- Stock-video explainer motion.
- Constant fade/slide animation.
- Every sentence becoming a separate graphic.
- Crowded board chaos.
- Empty placeholder boxes.
- Partial ugly caption reveals.
- Characters floating aimlessly.
- Props covering the lesson.
- Visuals implying AI is conscious or magical.
- Animation that exists only to keep the frame busy.

## Production Workflow

1. Define the lesson promise.
2. Write the voiceover.
3. Create an animatic using approved scene archetypes.
4. Review pacing with contact sheets.
5. Lock shot order and holds.
6. Polish only the scenes that survive the animatic.
7. Reuse character poses, props, and layouts before inventing new ones.

The animatic is the decision tool. Polish comes after the timing works.
